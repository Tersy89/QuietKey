How Enigma keeps secrets

Enigma is an Android app for sending encrypted messages and files through any messenger you already use. Keys are made on the phone, exchanged in person, and never touch a server. This page explains each layer, what it protects against, and where its limits are.

The big picture

Five layers between an attacker and your message

Each layer guards something different. A message on the move is protected by the bottom three; a message sitting on your phone is protected by the top two.

What each phone holds

Keys and what they do

Every member has a long-lived identity and a supply of short-lived keys. Every key comes in two halves from two unrelated families of mathematics, so a break in one family is not enough.

Signatures carry both halves: 64 bytes of Ed25519 followed by 3,309 bytes of ML-DSA-65. Both must verify or the packet is rejected.

Where keys come from

Randomness from the phone, its camera and its motion

Android's secure random generator is the foundation. Enigma adds a digest of five seconds of live camera frames from setup and group creation, and accelerometer and gyroscope readings gathered while the app is unlocked. HKDF mixes them, so extra inputs can only add unpredictability; they can never weaken the system generator.

Camera and motion input are a bonus, not a measured guarantee. Motion still contributes when the camera is broken, covered or dark. The microphone was rejected because it adds little and needs recording permission.

Building trust

Joining a group happens face to face

There is no key server and no directory. The owner's phone and the joining phone meet in person, connect over a local hotspot or the same Wi-Fi, and both screens show a six-digit check code that the two people compare before anything is accepted.

From a distance (1.1.0): the two people agree a passphrase of four or more words by voice, then swap two files over Signal or any app. The request holds only public keys; the invitation is encrypted to the joining phone. Each file carries a tag made with the passphrase (PBKDF2-SHA-256, 310,000 rounds, then HMAC), so a swapped file is refused. A request lasts seven days and works once.

Later members

When someone joins after you, the owner signs a new member list that carries one of your reserve keys for them and one of theirs for you. That list rides inside every message, so you learn about new members automatically and message them at full protection.

Two layers, kept apart

The connection key from the QR code only wraps the local link. It never stands in for the invitation, which is separately encrypted to the joining phone's own keys. Someone who photographs the QR code still cannot read the invitation.

One message, step by step

How a message is sealed

A message or file is encrypted once with a fresh content key. Then, for each chosen recipient (up to 50), that content key is locked under a key only that recipient can rebuild. The result is a single .enigma file, or ENG5: text, that can travel through any messenger.

Only chosen recipients

Each recipient finds an entry built from their own one-time key. Other group members know the group secret but not those private keys, so they see "not addressed to you".

No forwarding tricks

The signature covers the full recipient list and sits inside the encryption. A recipient cannot re-wrap your signed message for someone else and make it look addressed to them.

Stolen tomorrow, safe today

Forward secrecy: every key is used once

If someone records your messages now and steals your phone next year, the keys those old messages needed are already gone. Pools refill automatically: every message carries ten new keys back to the other person, with no time limit and no fixed count.

Protecting the phone itself

Two-step unlock, with a self-destruct

Opening Enigma takes something you know or are (passphrase or fingerprint) and then a 4-digit PIN, typed on Enigma's own number pad so no keyboard app ever sees it. The PIN is not a gate the app checks; it is part of the key. A copied vault file cannot be PIN-guessed on a computer, because the first step's secret is locked inside the phone's security hardware.

Wrong PINs 5 tries

Each attempt is written to storage before the PIN is checked, so closing the app does not reset the count. The screen shows how many attempts are left. A correct PIN resets it to five.

Panic PIN default 9999

Entering it instead of your PIN erases Enigma at once, then the app restarts as if freshly installed. Change it in Device → Security & unlock; the app warns while it is still 9999. It can never equal your real PIN.

Wrong PINs only count after the first step succeeds, so a stranger holding your phone cannot erase your data without your passphrase or fingerprint. Android does not let an app uninstall itself; "erase" means the keys and data are destroyed and the empty app remains.

Plainly stated

Limits

  • Enigma is a development prototype. Its design and code have unit and emulator tests, but no independent security audit.
  • Two algorithm families protect against a mathematical break. They do not protect against bugs in Enigma, Bouncy Castle or Android.
  • Forward secrecy depends on deleting keys. Flash storage and Java memory cannot guarantee that deleted bytes are gone.
  • Someone with root control of the phone who also has your passphrase or fingerprint could try all 10,000 PINs without triggering the wipe. The PIN protects a phone taken while running the normal Android system.
  • One-time and backup keys carry an Ed25519 signature only, to keep key pools small. They always travel inside a message or member list that carries both signatures.
  • Enigma holds Android's Internet permission only because the in-person joining link is a network socket. Messages never use the network: they leave the app only as files or text you share.
  • Enigma blocks screenshots of its own screens, but it cannot stop another camera.
  • Content is encrypted with AES-256-GCM alone. AES-256 is not considered at risk from quantum computers, so no second layer was added.
  • View once stops Enigma storing a message. It cannot stop someone photographing the screen with another device.
  • The group owner decides who joins. A dishonest or compromised owner can add devices; compare fingerprints when it matters.
  • Removing or blocking a member deletes the keys your phone holds for them (unless they are still in another of your groups). It does not yet replace the group secret on everyone's phones; for that, make a new group and invite the remaining members.
